A recruiter finds you on LinkedIn by searching for a job title and a technology, then decides in about ten seconds whether to open your profile properly. Almost everything that matters is what shows up in that search and in those ten seconds: your headline, your current role, and your location.

Most engineers optimise the wrong parts. This is what to fix, roughly in order of impact.

## The headline is a search field, not a slogan

By default, LinkedIn sets your headline to your current job title and company. That is already better than most of what people replace it with.

Recruiters search by keyword, and your headline is heavily weighted in that search. So it needs the words somebody would type.

```text
Weak:    Aspiring developer | Tech enthusiast | Lifelong learner
Weak:    Building cool things ✨
Better:  Backend Engineer | Python, Go, Postgres | Distributed Systems
Better:  Final-year CS student | Full-stack (React, Node) | Seeking 2027 new-grad roles
```

The rule: **job title plus three or four technologies plus, if you are looking, what you are looking for.** "Aspiring", "enthusiast" and "passionate" match no search anybody runs.

If you are a student, say so and say when you graduate. Recruiters filter new-grad pipelines by graduation year, and a profile that does not state one is a profile that does not appear.

## The About section: first two lines only

LinkedIn truncates About after roughly two lines on mobile with a "see more". Assume the rest is unread.

So lead with the definition, not the story:

```text
Backend engineer, three years on Python and Go services in production.
Most recently cut p95 latency 45% on an API serving 40k daily users.

[everything else, which fewer people will read]
```

Not:

```text
Ever since I was a child, I have been fascinated by technology...
```

Three to five short paragraphs, first person, no third-person narration about yourself. If your About section could describe eight other people, it is not doing anything.

## Experience: the same rules as your resume

Same discipline as [resume bullet points](/blogs/software-engineer-resume-bullet-points) - action verb, what you built, how, what changed - with two differences.

**You have more room here**, so one line of context about the team or product before the bullets is worth it. A reader who does not know the company needs to know what it does.

**Name technologies explicitly.** LinkedIn's search indexes this text. "Built the ingest pipeline" is fine prose and invisible to search; "built the ingest pipeline in Python with Kafka and Postgres" is both.

For projects with no employer, list them under Projects rather than inventing an Experience entry. Recruiters can tell, and a well-presented personal project is not something you need to disguise. [The portfolio guide](/blogs/software-engineering-portfolio-guide) covers how to present those so they carry weight.

## Skills, and why the order matters

LinkedIn shows your top three skills prominently and recruiters filter on them. Reorder so the three that matter for the roles you want are first - it is a drag-and-drop and it takes a minute.

Add real skills only. A list of forty makes the top three meaningless and makes you look like you added everything you have heard of. Fifteen to twenty is plenty.

Endorsements are weak signal and cost nothing; do not spend energy chasing them.

## Open to work: use the recruiter-only setting

LinkedIn has two versions of the open-to-work flag: the green photo frame that everyone sees, and a recruiter-only setting that is visible in LinkedIn Recruiter but not on your public profile. [LinkedIn's own help documentation](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a507508) covers how to set it.

If you are employed, the recruiter-only version is the one you want. If you are not, either is fine and the visible frame does genuinely surface you in more searches.

Fill in the job titles and locations properly. That is the field the filters actually run against, and a blank one means you do not appear.

## The parts people skip that actually matter

**Location.** Recruiters filter by it, always. A blank or vague location removes you from most searches. If you are open to relocating, say so in the About section.

**A photo.** Any reasonable photo. It does not have to be professional; it has to exist.

**Your custom URL.** `linkedin.com/in/yourname` rather than the default with a hash on the end. Free, takes thirty seconds, and it goes on your resume.

**The featured section.** Pin your best project, a deployed demo, or a post you wrote. It is the only part of the profile where you can put a link somebody will actually click.

## Posting, and whether it is worth it

Honest answer: it is optional, and it is oversold.

If you enjoy writing, posting occasionally about something specific you built - a bug you tracked down, a trade-off you made - does compound. It puts you in front of people who would not have searched for you.

If you do not enjoy it, do not force it. A well-structured profile with good project entries outperforms a badly written weekly post, and grinding out content you resent is a reliable way to stop doing either.

What is *not* worth it: engagement-bait posts, reposting motivational quotes, and commenting "Great post!" on things. Recruiters are not looking at your engagement rate.

## Connections and messages

**Connect with recruiters in your target companies.** Most accept. It costs nothing.

**When you send a note, be specific and short.** "I saw you are hiring backend engineers on the payments team - I have been building Go services on Postgres for two years and would like to be considered" is a good message. Three paragraphs about your journey is not.

**Referrals beat cold applications by a wide margin.** If you know somebody at a company, ask before you apply, not after. [The new-grad job guide](/blogs/new-grad-software-engineer-jobs) covers how to ask without it being awkward.

## A twenty-minute pass

If you do nothing else:

1. Rewrite the headline: title, three technologies, what you want.
2. Rewrite the first two lines of About so they define you.
3. Reorder your top three skills.
4. Set your location.
5. Claim your custom URL.
6. Pin one project to Featured.
7. Set open-to-work, recruiter-only if you are employed.

That is most of the available value. The remaining polish - recommendations, posting, the full About section - is real but it has a much worse ratio, and it is the part people start with.

Your LinkedIn does one job: get found, and survive the ten seconds after. Everything above serves one of those two.
