The entry-level engineering market is genuinely difficult right now, and pretending otherwise does not help anyone. New grad postings get hundreds of applicants within days. Companies that hired three juniors in 2021 now hire one mid-level engineer. Plenty of capable graduates are six months into a search with nothing to show for it.

None of that means it is impossible. It means the strategies that worked during the boom - apply broadly, wait - no longer work, and the ones that do work are mostly about timing, targeting and referrals rather than effort.

## Timing Is the Thing Nobody Tells You

**New grad pipelines open in August and September for roles starting the following summer.** Many close by October or November. If you start looking in your final semester in February, the large programmes have already filled.

This single fact costs more people offers than anything else on this page. The application cycle runs almost a year ahead of the start date, and nobody at university tells you.

A workable calendar for a final-year student:

| When | What |
|---|---|
| June - July | Resume finalised, portfolio deployed, referral list built |
| August - September | Apply to new grad programmes as they open |
| September - November | Interview loops, keep applying |
| December - February | Second wave: mid-size companies, startups |
| March onwards | Off-cycle roles, smaller companies, contract-to-hire |

The community-maintained [New Grad Positions repo](https://github.com/SimplifyJobs/New-Grad-Positions) tracks openings as they go live, which is the most practical way to catch programmes on the day they open rather than three weeks later.

If you still have a summer ahead of you, an internship is worth more than almost anything else you can do - many new grad seats are filled by returning interns before they are ever posted publicly. The same maintainers run an [internships repo](https://github.com/SimplifyJobs/Summer2027-Internships).

Recruiters find new-grad candidates by searching graduation year plus a technology, which makes your profile a discovery surface rather than a formality - [the LinkedIn guide](/blogs/linkedin-profile-software-engineer) covers what to fix.

## Referrals Convert, Cold Applications Mostly Do Not

This is the highest-leverage change most candidates can make, and most do not make it because asking feels presumptuous.

It is not. Referrals are how a large share of hiring actually happens, most companies pay employees a bonus for successful referrals, and referring someone costs an employee about four minutes.

**How to ask a stranger, in practice:**

Find engineers at the company on LinkedIn - ideally alumni of your university, which roughly doubles response rates. Send something short:

> Hi [Name] - I saw you work on [team] at [Company]. I am a final-year CS student at [University] applying for the [exact role, with req number if you have it]. I have built [one specific relevant thing, one clause]. Would you be open to referring me? Completely understand if not. Resume attached.

Why this works: it names the exact role, gives one concrete reason, includes the resume so there is nothing to chase, and gives them a graceful exit. What fails is the long message asking for "a quick chat to pick your brain" with no specific ask.

Send twenty. Expect three to five replies and one or two referrals. That is a normal, good outcome - the low response rate is structural, not personal.

**Also worth doing:** ask your university careers office which alumni are at which companies, ask former internship colleagues, and post publicly that you are looking. People cannot refer you if they do not know.

## Target Differently

Everyone applies to the same twenty companies. The maths is brutal there and much better elsewhere.

**Mid-sized companies (200-2,000 people)** hire consistently, run faster processes, and get a fraction of the applications. You will not have heard of most of them, which is exactly the point.

**Non-tech companies with real engineering teams.** Banks, insurers, retailers, logistics companies, healthcare, government. They hire graduates in volume, train them properly, and are chronically under-applied to because they are not exciting.

**Consultancies and agencies.** Sometimes lower pay and more process, but they hire juniors deliberately and you see many codebases in two years, which accelerates learning.

**Startups (Series A onwards).** Higher risk, less structure, more responsibility earlier. A Seed-stage startup rarely has capacity to train a junior; a Series B often does.

**Roles adjacent to the one you want.** QA automation, developer support, internal tools, technical implementation, solutions engineering. These are real engineering jobs with far less competition, and moving internally after eighteen months is dramatically easier than getting in from outside. A lot of good careers started here.

## Twenty Tailored Beats Two Hundred Generic

The instinct under pressure is volume. Volume without tailoring performs badly, because the filter that kills you is upstream of a human.

For each application worth making:

1. **Mirror the job description's language** for skills you genuinely have. Recruiters keyword-search their applicant tracking system, and if the listing says "REST APIs" while your resume says "web services", you do not appear in the search. The mechanics are in [the ATS resume guide](/blogs/ats-resume-software-engineer).
2. **Reorder your projects** so the most relevant one is first.
3. **Find a referral** before applying cold.
4. **Write a cover letter only if you have something specific to say** - for new grads, that usually means a genuine connection to the product or a gap to explain. [When it is worth writing one](/blogs/software-engineer-cover-letter) covers the judgement call.

Twenty of these will outperform two hundred untargeted submissions, and take less total time.

## What Actually Differentiates New Grads

Everyone applying has a degree and coursework. The differentiators are narrow:

**Internship experience.** The strongest single signal. If you have one, it goes at the top of the resume.

**Deployed projects with real users.** Not a tutorial clone. Something at a URL that someone other than you has used, with a README explaining what you decided and why. Three good projects, not twelve. [The portfolio guide](/blogs/software-engineering-portfolio-guide) covers what makes one count.

**Merged open source contributions.** Public evidence that you can work in a codebase you did not write and survive code review from someone with no reason to be nice. A handful of substantive merged PRs is worth more than a long list of typo fixes - [the first PR guide](/blogs/open-source-contribution-beginners) covers how to get there.

**A specific technical depth.** Being genuinely good at one thing beats being vaguely familiar with ten. "I know Postgres query planning well" is memorable; "familiar with SQL, MongoDB, Redis, Elasticsearch" is not.

## The Interview Loop

New grad loops are usually four stages, and each fails for different reasons.

**Online assessment.** Timed, automated, usually two or three algorithmic problems. Pure preparation - if you are failing OAs, the fix is [structured DSA practice](/blogs/dsa-study-plan-coding-interview), not more applications.

**Technical phone screen.** One or two problems, 45 minutes, with a human. The difference from the OA is that you have to talk while you think. Practise out loud; this is a different skill from solving silently and it is the one people neglect.

**Onsite / virtual onsite.** Two to four rounds: coding, sometimes a light system design round, and at least one behavioural. New grad system design is scoped down - they want to see that you understand components and trade-offs, not that you can design Twitter. [The system design roadmap](/blogs/system-design-interview-prep) covers the level expected.

**Behavioural.** Consistently the most under-prepared round, and a real source of rejections. University projects, hackathons, part-time work and open source are all legitimate material. Build six stories and structure them - [the STAR method guide](/blogs/star-method-interview-software-engineers) is the fastest way to make this round stop being a coin flip.

## Handling the Middle of a Long Search

The part nobody prepares you for is month four, when you have sent a hundred applications and had two rejections and ninety-eight silences.

**Silence is not feedback.** Most companies do not reply. It says nothing about you.

**Track the funnel, not the outcome.** Applications sent, replies, screens, onsites. If you are getting no replies, the problem is upstream - resume or targeting. If you get screens and no onsites, it is technical preparation. If you get onsites and no offers, it is usually behavioural or communication. Different problems, different fixes, and you cannot tell which without the numbers.

**Keep building while you search.** Six months of applying with nothing new to show makes the seventh month harder. Six months of applying while shipping a project and contributing to open source means your application is genuinely stronger each month.

**Three to six months is normal right now.** Knowing that in advance is worth a lot when you hit month four and start assuming you are the problem.

## The Short Version

Apply in August, not February. Ask twenty people for referrals and accept that most will not reply. Target companies nobody has heard of. Tailor twenty applications instead of firing off two hundred. Prepare the behavioural round like it counts, because it does. And keep shipping things while you wait, because the search is long and the only variable fully under your control is how much stronger you are in month six than you were in month one.
